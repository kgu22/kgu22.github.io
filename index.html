Job Market Paper

Admission, Not Prices: Export Processing Zones and the Allocation of Industrial Land in China

Place-based policies are usually judged by the activity they bring to the places they target, but where access to land is screened rather than priced, the value of a new zone depends on where its entrants would otherwise have located. Using China’s annual manufacturing survey on a 0.1-degree grid, I compare land at each distance from the 52 export processing zones (EPZs) opened in 2001–2007 with land at the same distance from the 69 special customs zones opened after 2007. Output on an EPZ’s own land rose 2.2-fold, while the other zones of its prefecture lost about 12 percent of their output.

An admission model estimated from where 105,600 new establishments located, in which firms rank zones by profit, zones admit by score, and prefectures set a priority for foreign-invested applicants, implies that 48–62 percent of the EPZ’s foreign-invested entrants would otherwise have entered those zones, and that the agglomeration it returned to them is worth 0.5–3.3 percent of their output. The priority for foreign-invested firms accounts for 53 percent of their admissions to national zones; competition among prefectures for mobile firms explains 10 percent of it.

Working Papers

Who Bears the Cost of a Zone? Local Protection across Prefecture Borders in China

A place-based policy reaches markets beyond the border of the government that adopts it, while that government’s means of helping firms stop at the border. I compare incumbent manufacturers at the same distance from the same export processing zone on the two sides of the host prefecture’s border, against those around zones not yet approved (zones opened 1998–2007). Neighboring incumbents become 8.9 percentage points less likely to survive than host incumbents, a gap concentrated among the most productive firms. Wages do not rise, and where an estimated admission model predicts more absorption, incumbents gain on both sides alike. The gap is a jurisdictional difference, not a market effect: it does not grow with the zone and holds between the two sides of the same stretch of border 10–25 km out.

Who Responds When Fertility Restrictions Are Lifted? Spousal Asymmetry and the Fertility Policy Margin in Rural China

I study which households respond when a household-wide fertility restriction is lifted, using the staggered second-birth eligibility of rural Chinese couples whose first child was a girl. Eligibility raised the share of couples with a second child by 15.5 percentage points after nine years, far less when the wife had completed senior high school; the husband’s schooling mattered much less. In an estimated dynamic fertility model, observed wage and career differences alone do not generate the negative gradient; allowing the latent net value of a second child to vary with the wife’s schooling reconciles the model with the observed responses.

Activity or Capability? Export Processing Zones and Portable Export Experience in China

Proposes a firm-level measure of export capability from customs data: whether experience helps a firm enter similar new markets. Processing experience in which the firm owns its imported materials carries about a third of the weight of ordinary exporting; experience processing consigned materials does not transfer. Five years after opening, zone exports equal 39 percent of nearby firms’ pre-opening exports, yet their portable experience changes by only 5 percent (s.e. 4).
