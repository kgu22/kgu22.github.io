Job Market Paper

Fenced Off: Export Processing Zones and the Local Economy in China

Place-based policies assume that activity concentrated in one place spreads to the economy around it. Export processing zones, the most common special economic zone, fence that activity off. I evaluate China’s 60 export processing zones, enclaves inside existing development zones whose exports peaked at US$130.2 billion, with the census of manufacturers, customs transactions, and business registrations. The event-study design tracks each location’s exposure to overlapping zones, and an estimated model describes how development zones ration admission.

Within 20 km of a zone, new domestic firms fell by 20 percent, concentrated among the smallest projects, even around fences that attracted almost no exports. Incumbent employment, wages, and foreign entry did not change. The fence took about 19 percent of the host zone’s admission slots and left little: domestic value added was at most 28 cents per exported dollar in the median zone, local suppliers did not expand, and nearby exporters shifted into processing that builds no portable capability. Unlike development zones that bundle land, incentives, and linkages, a fenced zone’s own activity says little about what it does to its host economy.

Working Papers

Who Bears the Cost of a Special Economic Zone? Incumbent Firms across Prefecture Borders in China

Compares incumbent manufacturers at the same distance from an EPZ on either side of the host prefecture’s border, using firm microdata geocoded to the village and a model of a commuting labor market with a home-biased host government. Host-side incumbents are shielded; across the border, incumbents 40–80 km away are 8.9 percentage points less likely to survive, and firms almost never relocate. The cost of a zone is exported down the regional wage gradient to jurisdictions that did not choose it.

Activity or Capability? Export Processing Zones and Portable Export Experience in China

Proposes a firm-level measure of export capability from customs data: whether experience helps a firm enter similar new markets. Processing experience in which the firm owns its imported materials carries about a third of the weight of ordinary exporting; experience processing consigned materials does not transfer. Five years after opening, zone exports equal 39 percent of nearby firms’ pre-opening exports, yet their portable experience changes by only 5 percent (s.e. 4).

Enclaves or Engines? Export Processing Zones and Local Production Linkages in China

Traces the backward-linkage chain with customs and manufacturing microdata, input–output tables, and stacked event studies. The domestic value-added ratio of fenced exports, an upper bound on local sourcing, was 0.27 in 2003; upstream supplier industries did not expand on average, and downstream processors did not respond when a 2005 reform opened sales across the fence. Physical concentration of activity did not become economic agglomeration.
