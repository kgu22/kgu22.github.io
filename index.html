Job Market Paper

Admission, Not Prices: Export Processing Zones and the Allocation of Industrial Land in China

China’s development zones sell industrial land at administered prices and choose their entrants by admission. I estimate a model of this market from where 105,600 new manufacturing establishments located: firms rank zones by profit, zones admit by score, and prefectures set a priority for foreign-invested applicants.

After an export processing zone (EPZ) opened, output on its own land rose 2.2-fold relative to the land of later customs zones, while the other zones of its prefecture lost output and foreign-invested firms; in the model, the EPZ took 48 percent of its foreign-invested entrants from them. Through agglomeration, its incumbents repaid those zones the output of the lost entrants in about 0.5 years. The foreign priority is mostly a valuation, with competition for mobile firms about a quarter of it; removing it would lower output by 1.5 billion yuan per host prefecture-year, mostly through the agglomeration that the admitted firms add.

Working Papers

Matching or Poaching? Local Government Competition for Manufacturing in China

Chinese prefectures compete for manufacturing through development zones, while land prices, taxes and the number of zones are set by higher levels of government. I compare manufacturers on the two sides of the host prefecture’s border around export processing zones opened in 1998–2007 with those around zones not yet approved. Host-side incumbents become more likely to survive, while neighboring incumbents lose 8.9 percentage points; the gap is largest where the neighbor is poorer, and new establishments are not cut by borders. In an estimated model, decentralized admission achieves 75 percent of a provincial planner’s gain. Competition is benign where rules fix prices and seats, and zero-sum where governments hold instruments that reach only their own firms.

Permission Is Not Enough: What Held Back Second Births after China’s Birth Quotas

When the state stops prohibiting a second child, what still prevents it? I study rural Chinese couples whose first child was a girl and who became eligible for a second child at dates set by provincial rules, comparing them with couples not yet eligible. Eligibility raised the annual probability of a second birth by 9.1 percentage points where the quota was enforced. The response was 11.7 points smaller when the wife, but not the husband, had completed senior high school, and small when permission came after the wife turned 30. A life-cycle model estimated on the same couples finds the money cost of children the largest remaining obstacle.

Activity or Capability? Export Processing Zones and Portable Export Experience in China

Proposes a firm-level measure of export capability from customs data: whether experience helps a firm enter similar new markets. Processing experience in which the firm owns its imported materials carries about a third of the weight of ordinary exporting; experience processing consigned materials does not transfer. Five years after opening, zone exports equal 39 percent of nearby firms’ pre-opening exports, yet their portable experience changes by only 5 percent (s.e. 4).

Enclaves or Engines? Export Processing Zones and Local Production Linkages in China

Traces the backward-linkage chain with customs and manufacturing microdata, input–output tables, and stacked event studies. The domestic value-added ratio of fenced exports, an upper bound on local sourcing, was 0.27 in 2003; upstream supplier industries did not expand on average, and downstream processors did not respond when a 2005 reform opened sales across the fence. Physical concentration of activity did not become economic agglomeration.
